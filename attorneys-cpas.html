The real problem

What is structurally different about a professional partnership

Professional firms have almost no hard assets. The value is the people, the client relationships, and the receivables — none of which survive a partner's death particularly well.

The buy-in was financed, and the debt is personal

Many partners borrow to fund a capital contribution. That obligation typically survives them, and it is rarely matched by a corresponding death benefit.

A cash-basis firm has nothing set aside

Professional firms distribute earnings rather than retain them. When the partnership agreement requires a buyout, the money has to come from somewhere — usually future profits of the surviving partners.

The agreement may be older than the firm's current value

Buy-sell formulas written a decade ago against a firm half the current size produce numbers nobody would accept today. Reviews are supposed to be periodic and almost never are.

Income is K-1, lumpy and self-employment taxed

Distributions vary with collections. That volatility makes fixed obligations riskier and makes the tax planning meaningfully harder than a W-2 household.

Deferred compensation from the firm is unsecured

Retired-partner payments, deferred capital returns and unfunded retirement arrangements are promises backed by the firm continuing to prosper.

Overhead does not stop when you do

If a partner is disabled, rent, staff salaries, malpractice premiums and the lease on the office keep running while their production goes to zero.

Personal liability sensitivity

Professionals in liability-exposed practices tend to care about the creditor treatment of their assets. Cash value protection varies materially by state and by policy ownership.

Very little qualified plan room relative to income

Even a well-designed cash balance plan has limits. Partners earning well into seven figures run out of tax-advantaged space quickly.

How the tools get used

The plays that fit a partnership

You will recognise most of these from the client side. The version that applies to your own firm is usually the one nobody has run the numbers on.

Do this first

Fund the buy-sell agreement you already signed

Read the agreement, value the firm honestly, and put policies in place sized to the obligation. Cross-purchase structures let surviving partners step up their basis but get unwieldy past three or four partners; entity-purchase is administratively simpler but does not provide the basis step-up. Trusteed and insurance-LLC arrangements sit in between. This choice has real tax consequences and should be made with the firm's CPA at the table.

Why it fits: An unfunded buyout obligation is not a plan. It is a promise that the surviving partners will find the money later, at the worst possible time.
Often missed

Business overhead expense (BOE) insurance

A short-duration disability product that reimburses the fixed costs of running your practice — rent, staff, utilities, insurance, lease payments — while you are unable to work. Distinct from personal disability coverage, which replaces your income.

Why it fits: For a solo practitioner or a small partnership, this is frequently the difference between a practice that survives an eighteen-month absence and one that closes.
Income protection

True own-occupation individual disability

Coverage that pays if you cannot practice in your own occupation, with a residual rider for a partial return and a future increase option so the benefit scales as your book grows. Group and association coverage is a reasonable base but rarely enough on its own.

Why it fits: For a partner whose income is entirely a function of billable capacity, this is the more probable claim by a wide margin.
Debt cover

Coverage sized to the buy-in note and personal guarantees

A straightforward term policy sized to the outstanding capital loan and any personal guarantees on the firm's line of credit, structured so the family can retire the obligation rather than negotiate it.

Why it fits: Cheap, unglamorous, and the item most likely to be missing when we review a new partner's situation.
Tax diversification

Permanent coverage as the bucket after the cash balance plan

Once a 401(k) and profit-sharing plan and, where the firm has one, a cash balance plan are fully used, a properly structured overfunded policy provides additional tax-deferred accumulation with access later through policy loans.

Why it fits: The relevant comparison is against a taxable brokerage account for money you do not intend to touch for fifteen or more years, in a top bracket, with a death benefit attached.
Retention

Golden handcuffs for the associates you cannot afford to lose

Executive bonus arrangements under Section 162, restrictive bonus agreements, and split-dollar plans all create a benefit that vests over time. The firm gets retention; the associate gets a portable asset at the end of the schedule.

Why it fits: Losing a senior associate with a portable book is a seven-figure event for a small firm. Retention economics are rarely close.
Succession

Funding an internal succession rather than a merger

Many firms merge because no internal buyer can finance the founder's exit. Insurance-backed installment structures and disability buy-out coverage make an internal transition financeable, which usually produces a better outcome for staff and clients.

Why it fits: Relevant from about ten years out, not two. The funding needs time to build.
Asset protection

Understanding the creditor treatment in your state

Many states provide some level of exemption for life insurance cash value and death benefits. The scope depends on your state, who owns the policy, and who the beneficiary is. It is a factor worth understanding, not a reason on its own to buy.

Why it fits: We flag it and refer the question to your own counsel — we do not practice law and will not pretend the analysis is simple.

Illustrative examples

What this looks like in practice

Hypothetical situations built to show how the pieces fit together. Yours will be different — that is the point of the call.

The new equity partner

A buy-in that does not become a widow's problem

Situation
Age 39, just made equity partner at a 12-attorney firm. Borrowed 400,000 for the capital contribution, repayable over seven years out of distributions. Two children under ten.
Strategy
A 10-year term policy sized to the declining note balance, layered under a longer 25-year policy sized to family income replacement, plus own-occupation disability with a future increase option. Separately, a review of whether the firm's buy-sell obligation is funded at all.
If the partner dies during the repayment period, the note is retired rather than inherited, and the family receives the capital account rather than a negotiation. The disability coverage addresses the more likely scenario, in which the partner survives but cannot bill.
The three-partner CPA firm

An agreement with money behind it

Situation
Three partners, ages 52, 58 and 61. The partnership agreement requires the firm to redeem a deceased partner's interest at a multiple of collections. Nothing is set aside. The oldest partner intends to retire in four years.
Strategy
Revalue the firm, update the formula, and put policies in place sized to the current obligation using a structure chosen with the firm's own CPA. Add disability buy-out coverage, since a long-term disability triggers a similar obligation and is statistically more likely.
The redemption obligation is funded rather than aspirational. The retiring partner's payout is not dependent on the two remaining partners having a strong four years, and the surviving partners are not funding a buyout out of the same cash flow they need to replace the lost production.

Straight answers

Questions we get from attorneys, cpas & partners

Our partnership agreement already covers what happens if a partner dies. Is that enough?

The agreement describes the obligation. It does not create the money. In most professional firms we look at, the agreement requires a buyout at a defined value and there is no funding mechanism other than "future profits." That means the surviving partners have to earn the deceased partner's buyout on top of replacing their production, at the exact moment the firm is least productive. Insurance is simply the cheapest way to have the cash arrive on the day the obligation does.

Cross-purchase or entity-purchase?

Cross-purchase gives the surviving partners a basis step-up in the interest they acquire, which can matter a great deal on a later sale, but it requires each partner to own a policy on every other partner — which becomes unmanageable past three or four. Entity-purchase is administratively simple but does not provide that step-up, and corporate-owned policies can have alternative minimum tax implications for C corporations. Trusteed cross-purchase and insurance-LLC arrangements try to get the best of both. This is genuinely a tax decision, and we make it with your CPA rather than for them.

What is business overhead expense insurance and why have I not heard of it?

It is a disability product that reimburses the fixed operating costs of your practice while you are disabled, typically for twelve to twenty-four months. It is not income replacement — that is your personal disability policy. It exists because a solo or small practice has ongoing costs that do not pause, and because the practice itself is often the most valuable thing the family owns. It is underwritten and priced separately, and it tends to be inexpensive relative to what it protects.

I am in a liability-exposed practice. Does life insurance help protect assets?

Possibly, and it depends heavily on your state and the ownership structure. Many states exempt some or all life insurance cash value and death benefit from creditors, but the scope, the caps and the treatment of who owns the policy vary widely. It should be a supporting consideration, not the reason for the purchase, and the analysis belongs with an attorney licensed where you practice. We will tell you what to ask them.

I already max a 401(k) and a cash balance plan. Is there any room left?

Not in qualified plans, which is exactly the situation permanent insurance is sometimes used to address. A properly structured, overfunded policy that stays within IRS premium limits offers tax-deferred growth and later access through policy loans. Whether it beats a low-turnover taxable portfolio depends on your bracket, your horizon and whether you also want the death benefit. We will model both, including the years where the policy looks worse.
