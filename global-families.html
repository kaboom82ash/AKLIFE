For immigrant, dual-national and cross-border households

The US estate tax rules for non-citizens surprise almost everyone who encounters them.

A surviving spouse who is not a US citizen does not get the unlimited marital deduction. A foreign national owning US property may face estate tax above a very small exemption. Families who built wealth across two countries often discover both facts at the worst possible time.

The real problem

Where cross-border households get caught

US estate and gift tax rules treat citizens, residents and non-residents very differently, and the differences are large enough to change outcomes by seven figures.

No unlimited marital deduction for a non-citizen spouse

Property passing to a surviving spouse who is not a US citizen does not qualify for the unlimited marital deduction the way it would between citizens. A qualified domestic trust, or QDOT, is the standard workaround, and it carries its own ongoing rules and a deferred rather than eliminated tax.

Non-resident aliens face a very small exemption

A non-resident, non-citizen who owns US situs assets — US real estate, and in many cases shares of US corporations — is generally subject to US estate tax with an exemption that is a tiny fraction of the amount available to citizens and residents.

Domicile is not the same as residency

US estate and gift tax turns on domicile, a facts-and-circumstances test, while income tax turns on residency. It is entirely possible to be a resident for one and not the other, and people plan against the wrong test all the time.

Support obligations that cross borders

Many households support parents or siblings overseas. Those obligations are real, ongoing, and would not survive the earner's death without a plan.

Foreign property is difficult to move or divide

An apartment in one country and a business in another may be governed by forced heirship rules, currency controls, or a probate process the US family cannot easily navigate.

Insurability has a geography

US carriers underwrite foreign nationals selectively, based on visa status, time in the country, ties, travel patterns and country of residence. What is available to you today may not be available after a status change or a move.

Currency and jurisdiction risk

A death benefit denominated in dollars, paid outside probate, is often the most portable and predictable asset a cross-border family can hold.

Advice does not usually cross borders either

The attorney in one country and the accountant in the other frequently have never spoken. Gaps and double-taxation both live in that space.

How the tools get used

What insurance solves in a cross-border plan

It does not solve the tax structure — that is the work of a cross-border attorney and an accountant qualified in both jurisdictions. What it does is fund the outcome their structure produces.

Cornerstone

Liquidity for a non-citizen spouse's estate exposure

Where a QDOT or another structure defers rather than eliminates tax, a death benefit provides the cash to satisfy the obligation without forcing a sale of the family home, a business interest, or investments held for the children.

Why it fits: The structure decides how much is owed and when. Insurance decides whether the family has to sell something to pay it.
Foreign nationals

Covering US situs estate tax exposure

A non-resident alien holding US real estate can face substantial US estate tax above a very small exemption. Life insurance is one of the more common funding solutions, and notably, life insurance proceeds on the life of a non-resident alien are generally not treated as US situs property.

Why it fits: Frequently the difference between heirs keeping a US property and having to sell it to pay a bill they did not expect.
Timing

Secure US underwriting while you qualify

Carriers assess foreign national applicants on visa category, time in the United States, financial and family ties, country of residence and travel patterns. Applying while you have a strong US presence typically produces better availability and pricing than applying after a move or a status change.

Why it fits: This is one of the few genuinely time-sensitive items in insurance planning, and it is entirely within your control today.
Family support

Securing support for parents and family abroad

A policy with clear beneficiary designations means that support to family overseas continues if you die. Payment mechanics, currency and the practicalities of paying a beneficiary in another country should be worked through in advance rather than discovered at claim time.

Why it fits: Naming an overseas beneficiary is straightforward; making sure they can actually receive and use the funds needs a little planning.
Portability

A dollar-denominated asset that passes outside probate

A death benefit paid to named beneficiaries avoids US probate, arrives in a stable currency, and does not depend on a foreign court recognising a US will or a US court interpreting foreign property law.

Why it fits: For families with assets in two or three jurisdictions, this simplicity is worth a great deal on its own.
Couples

Survivorship coverage where the second death is the taxable event

Where a structure defers the tax until the second death, a survivorship policy matches the timing of the liability and typically costs less than two individual policies.

Why it fits: The product should match when the money is actually needed. In a deferred structure, that is the second death.
Business

Cross-border business and partnership interests

Where a family business spans jurisdictions, buy-sell agreements, key person coverage and valuation provisions need to work under both legal systems and be funded in a currency the surviving parties can use.

Why it fits: Standard business planning, made considerably more complicated by two sets of rules.
Essential

Build a team that spans both countries

This planning genuinely requires a cross-border estate attorney and an accountant qualified in both jurisdictions, working from the same set of facts. Treaty positions, forced heirship, foreign trust reporting and gift tax on non-citizen spouses are all specialist territory.

Why it fits: We are the insurance and liquidity piece of that team, and we will say clearly when a question belongs to someone else.

What this looks like in practice

The mixed-citizenship couple

When the marital deduction does not apply

Situation
A US citizen aged 54 married to a spouse who holds a green card but has not naturalised. Combined estate of roughly 9 million, including a business interest and a home. Two children.
Strategy
A cross-border estate attorney evaluates a qualified domestic trust and the naturalisation question. A survivorship policy held in an irrevocable trust is sized to projected settlement costs, so the deferred obligation can be paid in cash.
The surviving spouse is not forced to sell the business interest or the home to satisfy a tax obligation the couple did not anticipate, and the children receive the intended assets rather than the residue after a distressed sale.
The foreign national investor

US real estate, non-US owner

Situation
A non-resident alien, age 60, owning three US rental properties worth about 4 million through direct ownership, with heirs living abroad.
Strategy
Cross-border counsel reviews the ownership structure. Life insurance is placed, where available given residence and travel profile, sized to the estimated US estate tax exposure above the very small non-resident exemption.
Heirs receive proceeds to pay the US estate tax bill and retain the properties, rather than having to liquidate US real estate under time pressure from another country and in another language.

Straight answers

Questions we get from global & cross-border families

My spouse has a green card. Does that make them a citizen for estate tax purposes?

No. The unlimited marital deduction generally applies only where the surviving spouse is a US citizen. A green card holder is a lawful permanent resident, which affects income tax residency, but it does not confer the marital deduction. The common approaches are a qualified domestic trust, which defers rather than eliminates the tax, or naturalisation before the first death where that is feasible and desired. Both are decisions for a cross-border estate attorney; our role is making sure there is liquidity for whatever obligation the chosen structure creates.

Can a non-US citizen buy US life insurance?

Often yes, and it depends on the carrier and your specific profile. Underwriters look at visa category, how long you have been in the United States, financial and family ties, your country of residence and citizenship, travel patterns, and whether there is a genuine US connection supporting the application. Availability and pricing vary considerably between carriers, which is where an independent advisor with access to several markets makes a practical difference. Applying while your US presence is strongest generally produces the best result.

I own a rental property in the US but I live abroad. Is that a problem?

It can be a significant one. A non-resident, non-citizen owning US situs assets — which includes US real estate — is generally subject to US estate tax above an exemption that is a small fraction of what citizens and residents receive. That can produce a large bill on a property the family cannot easily sell from overseas. Ownership structure matters and should be reviewed by cross-border counsel; insurance is the common way to fund the exposure that remains.

Will the death benefit be taxed in my home country too?

It depends entirely on that country's law and on whether a relevant estate or inheritance tax treaty applies. Some countries tax inheritances received by residents; some do not. The United States has estate and gift tax treaties with a limited number of countries. This is exactly the kind of question that requires an accountant qualified in both jurisdictions, and we would rather tell you that plainly than offer you a guess.

What if my status changes, or we move back?

A policy already in force generally stays in force — that is a large part of the argument for putting coverage in place while you clearly qualify. What can change is your ability to obtain new coverage, the servicing arrangements, and in some cases how premiums are paid from abroad. We will go through the specific carrier's rules on residence changes before you apply, so there are no surprises later.
