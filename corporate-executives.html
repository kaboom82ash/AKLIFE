The real problem

Where corporate benefits quietly fall short

Corporate benefits are designed for the median employee at your company. If you are well above that median, the design works against you in four or five predictable ways.

Group life is sized to the wrong number

One or two times base salary ignores bonus and equity. If half your compensation is variable, the coverage covers roughly half the loss.

It is also taxable, and it is not portable

Employer-paid group life above 50,000 dollars creates imputed income on your W-2 every year. And it terminates with employment — usually with a short, expensive conversion window you have days to act on.

Group disability has a ceiling

Most group LTD plans replace roughly 60 percent of base pay up to a monthly cap. For a high earner, that cap can mean the real replacement ratio is 30 or 40 percent — and if the employer pays the premium, the benefit is taxable when you collect.

Deferred comp is an unsecured promise

A non-qualified deferred compensation balance is a general creditor claim against your employer. It is not protected the way a 401(k) is. If the company fails, you stand in line.

Concentration you did not choose

Salary, bonus, equity, deferred comp and often an ESPP position all depend on the same company. That is a lot of household risk pointed at one balance sheet.

Nowhere left to put tax-advantaged dollars

Once the 401(k), mega-backdoor Roth if available, HSA and backdoor Roth are used, additional savings land in a taxable brokerage account and stay there.

Layoff risk is not hypothetical

Severance is finite. Health coverage changes. The individual policies you already own are the part of the plan that does not care about the reorg.

Two high earners, one estate

Dual-income households at this level can accumulate an estate large enough to matter, without ever thinking of themselves as a high-net-worth family.

How the tools get used

What we usually end up recommending

The theme is portability and tax diversification: build the parts of the plan your employer does not control.

Foundation

Portable individual term sized to total compensation

We size coverage to the household income actually being replaced — base plus a normalised bonus plus the annual value of vesting equity — and then subtract what is already in place. The result is almost always larger than the group policy, and it costs less than most people expect for a healthy 40-year-old.

Why it fits: This is the single change that survives a job change, a reorganisation, or a move to a startup.
Do this before you leave

Convert or replace group life before the window closes

Group policies typically offer a conversion right to an individual policy without medical underwriting, but the window is short and the conversion product is often expensive. Knowing whether to convert, replace, or let it go is a decision worth making before the resignation letter, not after.

Why it fits: If your health has changed since you were hired, that conversion right may be the most valuable benefit you have.
Income protection

Supplemental individual disability on top of group LTD

An individual policy layered over group coverage lifts the effective replacement ratio and, because you pay the premium with after-tax dollars, the benefit is generally received income-tax-free. It is also portable and cannot be changed by next year's benefits committee.

Why it fits: The higher your income, the bigger the gap between what group LTD replaces and what your household actually spends.
Tax diversification

A LIRP as the bucket after the 401(k) is full

An overfunded permanent policy, deliberately structured to avoid Modified Endowment Contract status, builds cash value on a tax-deferred basis. Later, policy loans can supplement retirement income without adding to taxable income — which also matters for Medicare IRMAA surcharges and the taxation of Social Security.

Why it fits: If everything you have saved is pre-tax, you have taken a large, unhedged bet on future tax rates.
Career flexibility

Cash value as a bridge between jobs

Accumulated cash value can be borrowed against quickly and without credit approval — a useful buffer during a severance gap, a sabbatical, a relocation, or the first uncertain year of a new venture.

Why it fits: The point is optionality. Having a liquidity source that is not your brokerage account changes what you can afford to say no to.
If you are being recruited

Knowing what to ask for in an executive package

Executive bonus arrangements under Section 162, split-dollar agreements and supplemental executive retirement plans are all negotiable pieces of a senior package. Understanding how each is taxed, who owns the policy, and what happens on separation puts you in a much stronger position at the table.

Why it fits: Most candidates negotiate salary and equity and accept the benefits section as written. That is leaving real money on the table.
Two-earner households

A survivorship policy for the estate that snuck up on you

A second-to-die policy covering both spouses pays after the second death and is typically cheaper than two individual policies. Held in an irrevocable trust, the proceeds can sit outside the taxable estate and give heirs cash to settle costs.

Why it fits: Relevant once a dual-income household with a paid-off house, two 401(k)s and a brokerage account starts approaching estate-tax territory in their state or federally.
Later-career

Long-term care coverage while you are still insurable

A hybrid policy or an acceleration rider covers qualifying long-term care costs and still pays a death benefit if care is never needed — which addresses the main objection people have to standalone LTC premiums.

Why it fits: Underwriting for LTC gets meaningfully harder after about 60. This is a decision with a shelf life.

Illustrative examples

What this looks like in practice

Hypothetical situations built to show how the pieces fit together. Yours will be different — that is the point of the call.

The director

Coverage that matches the actual paycheck

Situation
Age 45, a director at a public company. 280,000 base, a 30 percent target bonus, and roughly 150,000 a year in vesting RSUs. Group life is two times base — 560,000 against a household that spends against roughly 470,000 of income.
Strategy
A 20-year individual term policy layered on top of the group coverage, sized to the real replacement need and to the years until the youngest child finishes college. Supplemental individual disability added over group LTD. An overfunded IUL started with the annual bonus rather than the base salary.
The household is covered against actual income rather than base salary, the coverage moves with them to the next employer, and the bonus — the money most likely to be spent rather than saved — is redirected into a tax-advantaged bucket without touching the monthly budget.
The pre-exit executive

Deferred comp risk, and the conversion window

Situation
Age 56, a VP with 900,000 of non-qualified deferred compensation scheduled to pay out over ten years post-separation, planning to leave in about eighteen months. A heart condition diagnosed two years ago.
Strategy
Evaluate the group conversion right well before separation given the health history, layer permanent coverage where individual underwriting is still achievable, and use the death benefit as a partial hedge against the deferred comp balance.
If the executive dies before the deferred compensation is fully paid, the family is not dependent on the former employer remaining solvent for a decade. And the conversion decision is made with time to compare alternatives instead of inside a 31-day window.

Straight answers

Questions we get from corporate professionals

My company gives me free life insurance. Why would I buy more?

Three reasons. It is usually sized to base salary, which understates what your family actually lives on if you have significant bonus or equity. Employer-paid coverage above 50,000 dollars generates taxable imputed income each year. And it ends when your employment does — often at exactly the moment you are least able to replace it, because you are between jobs or because your health has changed. Keep the group coverage; it is free or nearly so. Just do not mistake it for a plan.

Should I count my RSUs when deciding how much coverage I need?

Yes, but carefully. We generally use a normalised annual vest value rather than the total unvested grant, because unvested shares are forfeited at termination and their value moves with the stock. The right way to think about it: how much after-tax income does your household actually consume each year, and for how many years would that need to continue?

Is my deferred compensation safe?

It is safe from you spending it, but it is not protected from your employer's creditors. A non-qualified deferred compensation plan is an unsecured promise to pay. That is fine for a financially strong company, and it is a real risk to think about if the balance is large, the payout period is long, and your employer's outlook is uncertain. Life insurance does not solve the solvency risk, but it can hedge the piece that only matters if you die before the payments finish.

I max out my 401(k) and do a backdoor Roth. What comes next?

In order, most people should first check whether their plan allows after-tax contributions and in-plan conversions — the mega-backdoor Roth — because that space is excellent and often unused. Then an HSA if you are eligible, treated as a retirement account rather than a spending account. After that it is a choice between a taxable brokerage account and a properly structured permanent policy, and the honest answer depends on your time horizon, your tax bracket now versus later, and whether you also need the death benefit. We will show you both sides.

What should I do about my coverage before I resign?

Ideally, put individual coverage in force before you give notice, while you are still employed and insurable. Then review your group conversion rights, understand the deadline in writing, and check whether any executive arrangements — split dollar, an executive bonus policy, a SERP — have separation provisions that change who owns what. This is a short conversation that is much less useful after the fact.
