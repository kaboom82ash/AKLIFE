For founders and owners handing the business to the next generation

You built something worth passing on. The hard part is passing it on intact.

Most of your net worth is inside one illiquid company. Your heirs will owe cash on a deadline. One child works in the business and the others do not. And your retirement income depends on a company you are about to stop running. These problems are solvable, but not quickly — they need lead time.

  • Estate liquidity so heirs pay settlement costs in cash, not by selling the company
  • A fair outcome for the children who are not in the business
  • Retirement income that does not require you to sell to a stranger

The real problem

Why succession fails, and it is almost never about the business

The company is usually fine. What breaks is the liquidity, the fairness, and the timing.

Illiquid value, liquid obligations

Estate settlement costs, taxes and buyout obligations are payable in cash on a schedule. A closely held business converts to cash slowly, at a discount, and only if a buyer happens to be available.

A forced sale is a discounted sale

Every professional buyer knows what a distressed estate looks like. Selling on someone else's timeline reliably costs a meaningful share of the value you spent thirty years building.

One child is in it, the others are not

Dividing the business equally hands non-operating children an illiquid minority stake with no control and no distributions. Dividing it unequally without a plan feels like favouritism. Both paths end badly without a third option.

Your retirement is the company

If your income depends on distributions, a sale price, or a note from the next generation, then your retirement security is a bet on the business continuing to perform without you running it.

An unfunded buy-sell is just a document

If there are co-owners, the agreement describes a buyout obligation. Without funding, the survivors have to produce the cash from operations, borrowing, or a sale.

Key people leave during transitions

The general manager, the head of sales, the operations lead — the people who make the handoff possible — are also the most employable, and they are watching the transition closely.

Valuation is contested at exactly the wrong time

A business valued informally for years can face an aggressive valuation for estate tax purposes. Documentation, formulas and appraisals matter more than owners expect.

Nobody wants to talk about it

The single most common finding is that the founder has a rough plan in their head, the children each have a different understanding of it, and nothing has been said out loud.

How the tools get used

How life insurance carries a succession plan

Insurance rarely solves the strategy. What it does is make the strategy financeable — it puts cash in the right hands, on the right day, without requiring the business to be sold.

Cornerstone

Estate liquidity through a policy held in an ILIT

A policy owned by an irrevocable life insurance trust, rather than by you, means the death benefit is generally not included in your taxable estate. The trust receives cash that the estate can access by buying assets from it or lending to it, so settlement costs are paid without touching the business.

Why it fits: The classic use case, and the one most likely to preserve the company intact through the settlement period.
Cost efficiency

Survivorship (second-to-die) coverage

A policy on two lives that pays after the second death — which, for a married couple, is usually when the estate tax and settlement costs actually arrive. Because it insures two lives, it typically costs meaningfully less than two individual policies, and underwriting can be more forgiving if one spouse has health issues.

Why it fits: This is the standard product for the estate liquidity job, for exactly the reason that the need is second-death.
Fairness

Inheritance equalisation

The operating child receives the business. The non-operating children receive a death benefit of comparable value. Nobody is forced into an unwanted partnership, nobody has to be bought out, and the estate does not have to be carved up into pieces that damage the company.

Why it fits: Cheaper, cleaner and far less destructive than any of the alternatives — including the popular one of leaving it to the children to work out.
Transfer funding

Securing an installment sale or a gifting programme

If you are selling to the next generation over time, the note should be insured on both lives. Coverage on the buyer means the balance is retired if they die. Coverage on you means cash arrives in your estate to settle or equalise without pressuring the business for early payment.

Why it fits: Transfers structured over ten to fifteen years pass through a lot of life. The funding should not depend on nothing happening.
Retention

Golden handcuffs for the people who make the handoff work

Executive bonus arrangements under Section 162, restrictive bonus agreements, split-dollar plans and supplemental executive retirement plans all create a benefit that vests over a schedule. The message to a key manager is concrete: stay through the transition and this is yours.

Why it fits: Cheap insurance against the operations lead leaving in month four of a three-year handoff.
Your income

A retirement stream that does not require a sale

A combination of an overfunded policy built over the preceding years, an income annuity, and a structured note from the buyer can create a retirement income floor. The goal is that you can retire because you want to, not because you had to find a buyer.

Why it fits: This one needs the most lead time. Ten years out is comfortable; two years out limits the options considerably.
Charitable

A wealth replacement trust alongside a charitable gift

If part of the plan is giving an asset to a charity or a donor-advised fund, a life insurance policy held in trust can replace that value for your heirs — so the gift does not come at their expense.

Why it fits: Common for founders who want a named legacy and also want the children whole.
Large cases

Premium financing, used carefully

Borrowing from a bank to pay premiums on a large policy preserves working capital in the business. It also introduces interest rate risk, collateral requirements and ongoing lender relationships that need to be modelled honestly, including in scenarios where rates rise and performance disappoints.

Why it fits: Occasionally the right answer for a large case. We will show you the downside illustrations, not just the favourable ones.

Illustrative examples

What this looks like in practice

Hypothetical situations built to show how the pieces fit together. Yours will be different — that is the point of the call.

The founder

Three children, one company, no forced sale

Situation
Age 63, owns a manufacturing business valued at roughly 12 million, plus a building held in a separate LLC. Three children: one runs operations and will take over, two are not involved. The business is roughly 80 percent of the estate.
Strategy
A survivorship policy on both parents held in an irrevocable life insurance trust, sized to projected settlement costs plus the equalisation need. A gifting programme moving non-voting interests to the operating child over time, designed with the attorney and a qualified appraiser. Executive bonus arrangements for two key managers vesting over the transition period.
At the second death, the trust receives a death benefit outside the taxable estate. The operating child owns the company. The two non-operating children receive comparable liquid value. Settlement costs are paid from insurance proceeds rather than by selling equipment, the building, or the company itself.
The owner with a partner

An agreement with money behind it

Situation
Age 58, co-owns a commercial services company 60/40 with a partner who is 66 and wants out within five years. The buy-sell requires the company to redeem a departing or deceased owner's interest. Nothing is funded.
Strategy
Revalue the business, update the agreement, and fund the death and disability triggers with policies structured with the company's CPA. Plan the voluntary retirement buyout separately, since insurance funds the involuntary triggers and a note or sinking fund handles the planned one.
The company is not exposed to funding a redemption out of operating cash flow at the same moment it loses a principal. The remaining owner keeps control, and the departing partner's family receives an agreed price rather than a negotiation.

These are hypothetical examples for illustration only, not guarantees, projections, or recommendations for any specific policy or person. Dollar figures are round numbers chosen to make a point, not quotes. Actual outcomes depend on the policy you choose, your health and underwriting, how the policy is funded and structured, and current tax law, which can change. Straight answers

Questions we get from founders passing the torch

Will my estate actually owe federal estate tax?

It depends on the size of your estate and the exemption in effect at the time of your death — and the federal exemption amount has changed repeatedly and is scheduled to change again. Several states also impose their own estate or inheritance tax at much lower thresholds than the federal one. The honest answer is that nobody can tell you what the law will be when it matters, which is precisely the argument for building liquidity that works under more than one version of the rules. Your CPA and estate attorney should run the current numbers; we plan the liquidity.

Why put the policy in a trust instead of just owning it?

If you own a policy on your own life, the death benefit is generally included in your taxable estate — which is self-defeating when the whole purpose is to pay estate costs. A properly drafted and administered irrevocable life insurance trust owns the policy instead, so the proceeds are generally excluded. It has to be done correctly: the trust should ideally be the original owner or the transfer must survive a three-year lookback, premiums are usually funded through gifts with Crummey notices, and the trustee has real duties. Your attorney drafts it; we handle the policy.

My children will just work it out. Why formalise anything?

They might. But they will be doing it while grieving, with a lawyer each, and with spouses who have their own views and no shared history with the business. The families that stay intact are the ones where the founder said out loud what the plan was, wrote it down, and funded it. The cost of formalising it is a few conversations and some premium. The cost of not doing it is frequently the business and the relationships.

Can I fund this without pulling cash out of the business?

Sometimes, and there are several approaches. The business itself can own key person coverage. An executive bonus arrangement can move premium dollars in a tax-efficient way. Split-dollar can share cost between the company and the owner. For large cases, premium financing borrows from a bank rather than from operations. Each has trade-offs — tax treatment, ownership, control on exit, and in the financing case, real interest rate and collateral risk. We would rather show you three structures with honest numbers than push one.

How far ahead should I start?

Ten years before the intended handoff is comfortable. Five is workable. Two is constrained, mostly because underwriting gets harder with age and health, and because gifting and valuation strategies need time to be effective. The single most common regret we hear is not that someone started too early.

What if I would rather sell to a third party than to my family?

Then the planning changes but does not disappear. You still need key person retention through a sale process, you still need coverage during any earn-out or seller-note period, and you will have a much larger liquid estate afterwards — which usually creates a bigger estate liquidity question, not a smaller one. Several of the strategies on this page apply either way, which is a good reason not to wait until the decision is final.
