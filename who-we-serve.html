Planning by profession and situation

The right plan depends entirely on who you are

A surgeon with 240,000 in private student loans, a founder handing a company to one of three children, and a landlord with 2.8 million in personally guaranteed debt all need life insurance. They need almost nothing else in common. These pages lay out what actually applies to each situation — the specific risks, the strategies that fit, and the questions worth asking before you buy anything.

Do not see yourself here? The consultation is free either way — we will tell you honestly if we are not the right fit.
Professionals & high earners

High income, high tax, thin employer coverage

The common thread: compensation that outgrows the benefits package built on top of it, and very little tax-advantaged room left once the standard accounts are full.

Physicians, dentists, NPs, PAs, allied health

Doctors, Nurses & Specialists

Clinicians start earning late, carry more debt than almost anyone, and depend on a very specific set of skills staying intact.

Managers, directors, VPs and executives

Corporate Professionals

Corporate compensation is increasingly bonus, equity and deferred — but the benefits built on top of it are still sized to base salary, taxed in ways people do not expect, and gone the day you leave.

RSUs, options, pre-IPO and post-liquidity

Tech & Equity Comp

Equity compensation creates a very specific pattern: enormous paper wealth, very little liquidity, a tax bill with hard deadlines, and life insurance sized to a base salary that is a fraction of what you actually earn.

Professional practice partners and owners

Attorneys, CPAs & Partners

Partnership capital accounts, buy-in debt, K-1 income, an unsecured deferred compensation promise, and an agreement that describes a buyout without funding one.

Business owners & operators

Wealth locked inside something you cannot sell quickly

Whether you are taking the business over, handing it down, or building it out, the problems rhyme: illiquid value, personal guarantees, key people, and heirs who want different things.

Taking over the family business

Next-Gen Business Owners

A family business transfer looks like a handshake and is actually a financed acquisition, a key person problem, a sibling fairness question, and a personal guarantee — all at once.

Succession, estate liquidity and legacy

Founders Passing the Torch

Most of your net worth is inside one illiquid company. Your heirs will owe cash on a deadline. One child works in the business and the others do not.

Franchisees, operators and area developers

Franchise & Multi-Unit

Franchising concentrates three obligations most owners handle by default: a lender who requires life insurance, a franchisor who requires a continuity plan, and an operation that runs on a small number of managers you cannot easily replace.

Landlords, syndicators and developers

Real Estate Investors

Asset-heavy & specialised situations

Where the standard playbook does not apply at all

Land that cannot be divided, careers that end at 32, estates that cross borders, and the decade before retirement where every decision affects the next thirty years.

Land-rich, cash-poor, and multi-generational

Farm & Ranch Families

Ground that has been in the family for generations is worth far more on paper than it produces in cash.

Short careers, concentrated earnings

Athletes & Entertainers

Compressed earning windows change every assumption in conventional financial advice. The risk is not a bad market — it is a career that ends abruptly, a lifestyle built on peak income, and no plan for the thirty years after the money stops.

See the plan for this group →
Non-citizen spouses, foreign nationals, US assets

Global & Cross-Border Families

A surviving spouse who is not a US citizen does not get the unlimited marital deduction. A foreign national owning US property may face estate tax above a very small exemption.

See the plan for this group →
Large IRAs, a recent sale, or five years out

Pre-Retirees & Recent Exits

A large pre-tax balance is a partnership with the IRS whose terms have not been set yet. Add required distributions, Medicare surcharges, the ten-year rule for your children, a term policy about to expire, and the cost of care — and the decade before retirement turns out to be the one that matters most.

What every one of these pages has in common

The strategies differ enormously. The approach does not: understand the situation first, explain the trade-offs honestly, and only then talk about products.

  • We are independent, so the recommendation is not constrained to one carrier's shelf
  • Every strategy comes with its downside explained, not just its illustration
  • Tax and legal structure belongs to your attorney and CPA — we fund the plan they design
  • If a simple term policy is the right answer, that is what we will tell you
