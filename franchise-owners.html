For franchisees, multi-unit operators and area developers

Your franchise agreement already requires a succession plan. Most owners have never read that clause.

Franchising concentrates three obligations most owners handle by default: a lender who requires life insurance, a franchisor who requires a continuity plan, and an operation that runs on a small number of managers you cannot easily replace.

  • A continuity plan your franchisor will actually approve

The real problem

Three parties have a say in what happens to your business

A franchisee owns the operation but not the brand, and both the lender and the franchisor have contractual rights that activate on a death, a disability or a transfer.

The SBA loan requires life insurance

SBA 7(a) lending commonly requires a collaterally assigned life insurance policy on the principal, particularly where the business is heavily dependent on that individual. Owners frequently buy whatever the lender puts in front of them.

The franchise agreement controls the transfer

Most franchise agreements require franchisor approval for any transfer, including a transfer to heirs, and many require a designated successor who meets the brand's qualification standards. A spouse who has never run a unit may not qualify.

Growth eats cash

Every additional unit is a build-out, equipment, working capital and a ramp period. Multi-unit operators are structurally capital-hungry and structurally dependent on lenders.

A few managers hold it together

The general managers and the area supervisor are what let an owner operate multiple locations. Their departure is an operational emergency, and the labour market for good operators is permanently tight.

Owner dependence is real

Lenders and franchisors both assess whether the business runs without the principal. In most single-owner franchise groups, the honest answer is not for very long.

Partners without a written exit

Many franchise groups are owned by two or three partners who put money in together and never documented what happens if one of them dies, divorces or wants out.

Personal guarantees on everything

Loans, leases, and often the franchise agreement itself are personally guaranteed. A multi-unit operator can have guarantee exposure well beyond their equity.

Royalties and lease obligations continue

Revenue can drop sharply during a disruption. Percentage rent, royalties, marketing fund contributions and lease payments largely do not.

How the tools get used

The franchise owner's short list

Two of these are usually already required by someone else. The others are what turn a compliance exercise into an actual plan.

Do it right the first time

Buy the lender-required policy on your terms

When a lender requires collateral assignment, the requirement is that a policy be assigned — not that you buy a specific product from a specific source. A properly sized term policy, assigned to the loan and released as it amortises, satisfies the requirement and leaves you owning coverage after the loan is gone.

Why it fits: This is a requirement you will meet anyway. Meeting it deliberately usually costs less and leaves you with a better asset.
Contractual

A continuity plan the franchisor will approve

Identify a designated successor who can qualify under the brand's standards, document it, and fund the transition — whether that means a family member being trained, a manager being groomed to buy, or a broker-led sale with enough liquidity to run the units in the interim.

Why it fits: Franchisors care a great deal about this and will usually engage constructively if you raise it. They care much less once they are dealing with an estate.
Operations

Key person coverage on the operators who matter

Coverage owned by the business on the multi-unit manager or area supervisor whose departure would materially damage performance. The proceeds fund recruiting, a retention bonus pool for the rest of the team, and the ramp period for a replacement.

Why it fits: Note the notice and consent requirements for employer-owned life insurance — done incorrectly, an otherwise tax-free benefit can become taxable.
Retention

Executive bonus plans to hold general managers

A Section 162 bonus arrangement, often with a restrictive endorsement, pays premiums on a policy the manager owns and vests over a schedule. It is far simpler than a formal deferred compensation plan and gives a good GM a concrete reason to stay for the next five years.

Why it fits: In an industry where good operators are recruited constantly, this is one of the cheapest retention tools available.
Partnerships

A funded buy-sell among the owners

A written agreement setting the trigger events, the valuation method and the funding source, so a deceased owner's family receives a fair price in cash and the surviving owners keep control of the units and the franchise relationship.

Why it fits: Franchisor approval requirements make an unplanned ownership change much messier here than in an independent business.
Growth capital

Cash value as build-out funding

Cash value accumulated in a permanent policy can be borrowed against to fund a build-out, an equipment package, or working capital during a ramp, without a new credit application and without adding covenants.

Why it fits: Useful for the operator who has discovered that unit five is harder to finance than unit two because of aggregate guarantee exposure.
Income protection

Disability and business overhead coverage

Personal disability coverage replaces the owner's income. Business overhead expense coverage reimburses rent, royalties, payroll and lease payments during a disability, and disability buy-out coverage funds a purchase of the interest if the condition is permanent.

Why it fits: Fixed obligations in franchising are unusually high relative to margin, which makes an owner's extended absence expensive fast.
Exit

Planning the sale before the broker calls

Multi-unit groups sell on a multiple of adjusted cash flow, and buyers discount heavily for owner dependence and thin management. The same investments that reduce your key person risk also raise your multiple.

Why it fits: Retention plans and documented management depth are simultaneously a risk fix and a valuation strategy.

Illustrative examples

What this looks like in practice

Hypothetical situations built to show how the pieces fit together. Yours will be different — that is the point of the call.

The multi-unit operator

Six locations, two lenders, one owner

Situation
Age 44, owns six quick-service locations built over nine years, roughly 3.1 million in SBA and conventional debt, all personally guaranteed. Spouse works outside the business. The franchisor requires an approved successor.
Strategy
Term coverage sized to total guaranteed debt with proper collateral assignments to each lender, key person coverage on the area supervisor, a Section 162 bonus arrangement vesting over five years for the two strongest general managers, and a written continuity plan filed with the franchisor.
If the owner dies, the debt can be retired rather than called, the franchisor has an approved plan on file instead of an estate to negotiate with, and the management team has a financial reason to stay through the transition — which preserves the value of the units whether the family keeps or sells them.
The two-partner group

When one partner's spouse inherits half the business

Situation
Two partners, ages 39 and 52, owning four home services franchises 50/50. No buy-sell agreement. The older partner's spouse has no involvement and no interest in the business.
Strategy
A cross-purchase buy-sell with an agreed valuation formula, funded with policies each partner owns on the other, plus disability buy-out coverage for the more probable scenario.
On a death, the surviving partner buys the interest at a pre-agreed price with insurance proceeds. The family receives cash promptly rather than an illiquid half-interest, and the franchisor deals with a single qualified owner rather than an approval problem.

Straight answers

Questions we get from franchise & multi-unit

The bank arranged a policy when I closed my SBA loan. Is that fine?

It is often fine as compliance and frequently poor as planning. Lender-arranged coverage is sometimes more expensive, sometimes sized to the full loan for its entire term rather than the amortising balance, and sometimes structured so you have nothing left once the loan is repaid. It is worth a review: you may be able to satisfy the same requirement with better coverage you keep afterwards.

What does my franchisor actually require if I die?

It varies by brand, but most agreements require franchisor consent for any transfer, including to a spouse or child, and many require the successor to complete the brand's training and meet its financial qualification standards within a set period. Some reserve a right of first refusal. Read your specific agreement — and then design the plan around what it says, because the franchisor is not going to waive it during an estate settlement.

Can the business pay for coverage on my general manager?

Yes, and it is common. Two things matter. First, employer-owned life insurance rules require written notice to and consent from the insured employee before the policy is issued, along with certain reporting — failing this can make the death benefit taxable to the business. Second, be clear about whether the arrangement is pure key person coverage, which benefits the company, or a retention benefit for the employee, which is usually structured as a Section 162 bonus with the employee owning the policy. They serve different purposes and are taxed differently.

I want to open two more units. Can insurance help fund that?

Indirectly, and only if you started years ago. Cash value that has accumulated in a permanent policy can be borrowed against for a build-out without a new credit application, which is genuinely useful once aggregate guarantee exposure starts making lenders cautious. What it is not is a way to fund growth from a standing start — it takes time to build. If you are two units away from a lender limit, the more immediate answer is usually improving management depth so the units underwrite better.

Does any of this make my business worth more?

Yes, and the effect is not small. Buyers of multi-unit franchise groups discount heavily for owner dependence, thin management, and undocumented succession. Retention arrangements that keep proven general managers, a documented continuity plan, and a clean buy-sell all reduce perceived risk. The same investments that protect your family also tend to raise your multiple.
