The real problem

The risks that only show up in this decade

Most of these were irrelevant at 45 and are urgent at 60. They also interact: a decision about one changes the answer to the others.

A large pre-tax balance is a deferred tax bill

Every dollar in a traditional 401(k) or IRA is taxed as ordinary income when it comes out — by you, or by your heirs. The balance you see on the statement is not the balance you own.

Required distributions force income you may not want

Once required minimum distributions begin, taxable income arrives whether you need it or not, which can push you into higher brackets and interact badly with everything below.

Medicare surcharges are a cliff, not a slope

IRMAA surcharges on Medicare premiums are determined by income from two years prior and step up at thresholds. Crossing one by a small amount costs the same as crossing it by a lot.

The stretch IRA is gone for most heirs

Under the SECURE Act, most non-spouse beneficiaries must empty an inherited retirement account within ten years. Adult children frequently inherit during their own peak earning years, at their own top bracket.

Sequence-of-returns risk is concentrated

A poor market in the first few years of withdrawals does disproportionate, permanent damage to a portfolio compared with the same loss ten years later.

Care costs are the largest unbudgeted risk

Several years of assisted living or nursing care for one or both spouses can consume a large share of a portfolio that was otherwise adequate.

The survivor gets a worse deal

When one spouse dies, one Social Security benefit stops, a pension may reduce, and the survivor files as a single taxpayer — narrower brackets on similar income.

Term policies expire on schedule

A 20-year term bought at 45 ends at 65 — frequently right when estate liquidity and legacy considerations are becoming relevant, and when replacing coverage is most expensive.

How the tools get used

The moves that are still available in this window

Most of these close or become materially more expensive after 70. That is what makes this decade the planning decade.

Highest impact

Roth conversions paired with life insurance as the legacy asset

Converting portions of a pre-tax balance in the gap years — after employment income stops and before required distributions and Social Security begin — moves money into a tax-free bucket at a bracket you choose. Life insurance complements this: the death benefit passes to heirs generally income-tax-free with no ten-year drawdown requirement, which is precisely what the SECURE Act took away.

Why it fits: For anyone whose children will inherit a large IRA while in their own peak earning years, this pairing is usually the single most valuable conversation available.
Do not delay

Long-term care coverage while you are still insurable

A hybrid life policy with a long-term care rider, or a standalone policy, funds extended care and — unlike traditional standalone coverage — leaves a death benefit if care is never needed. Underwriting becomes materially harder after about 60, and a single diagnosis can close the door permanently.

Why it fits: This is the item with the hardest deadline on the page. Health, not age, is what ends the opportunity.
Pension decision

Pension maximisation, run honestly

If you have a defined benefit pension, the single-life option pays more than the joint-and-survivor option. Taking the higher payment and insuring the survivor benefit can produce a better outcome — or a much worse one if the insurance is not permanent, is not adequately funded, or if health changes. It requires real modelling, including the scenario where the plan does not work.

Why it fits: This is a genuinely useful strategy that has been sold badly often enough to deserve scepticism. We will show you both columns.
Income floor

An annuity to cover the non-negotiable expenses

Converting a portion of the portfolio into contractual lifetime income covers the fixed costs — housing, food, healthcare, insurance — so market volatility only affects discretionary spending. That structurally reduces sequence-of-returns risk in the years it does the most damage.

Why it fits: The right question is not whether annuities are good, but which specific expenses you want covered no matter what happens.
Deadline

Convert term coverage before the window closes

Most term policies include a conversion right to permanent coverage without new medical underwriting, but it expires — often at a stated age or a set number of years, whichever comes first. If your health has changed since you bought it, that right may be more valuable than the policy itself.

Why it fits: Check the actual contract, not your recollection of it. This is a date-certain deadline that people miss routinely.
Estate

Survivorship coverage in an ILIT for settlement costs

For estates approaching a state or federal threshold, a second-to-die policy owned by an irrevocable trust delivers cash at the second death, generally outside the taxable estate, so heirs pay costs without liquidating a business, a property, or a concentrated position.

Why it fits: Note that several states impose estate or inheritance tax at thresholds far below the federal exemption.
Charitable

Qualified charitable distributions and wealth replacement

Once eligible, qualified charitable distributions let you satisfy required distributions by giving directly to charity, excluding the amount from income entirely. Where a larger charitable gift is planned, a life insurance policy in trust can replace that value for heirs.

Why it fits: One of the few strategies that improves your tax position and your charitable impact simultaneously.
Recently liquid

After a sale, the questions change

A liquidity event converts a concentrated illiquid position into a taxable, investable and now clearly measurable estate. The priorities become preservation, tax efficiency, an income floor, and transfer planning — often for the first time at a scale where estate tax is genuinely in play.

Why it fits: The most common error is running the accumulation-phase plan for another five years out of habit.

Illustrative examples

What this looks like in practice

Hypothetical situations built to show how the pieces fit together. Yours will be different — that is the point of the call.

The corporate retiree

Defusing a 2.4 million pre-tax balance

Situation
Age 61, retiring at 63. 2.4 million in a traditional 401(k), 600,000 in taxable accounts, and a paid-off home. Two adult children, both high earners in their forties.
Strategy
Partial Roth conversions in the gap years between retirement and the start of Social Security and required distributions, sized each year to fill a target bracket without crossing an IRMAA threshold. A permanent policy funded partly from the taxable account, positioned as the tax-free legacy asset. A hybrid long-term care policy underwritten now.
Less of the estate is left in a pre-tax account that the children would have to empty within ten years at their own top marginal rate. Care costs have a dedicated funding source rather than being a claim on the portfolio. And the death benefit passes income-tax-free, outside probate, with no drawdown deadline attached.
The recently exited owner

From one illiquid asset to a visible estate

Situation
Age 58, sold a business for roughly 8 million after tax. Three children, a charitable intent, and a term policy expiring in two years.
Strategy
Convert the expiring term to permanent coverage before the conversion right lapses. Establish an irrevocable trust with counsel and fund a survivorship policy for estate liquidity. Build an income floor with a portion of proceeds, and structure the charitable gift alongside a wealth replacement policy.
The coverage that was about to disappear becomes permanent while still insurable. Heirs receive liquidity outside the taxable estate. The charitable intent is fulfilled without reducing what the children receive, and fixed living expenses no longer depend on market performance.

Straight answers

Questions we get from pre-retirees & recent exits

Why would I buy life insurance at 60? My kids are grown.

The reason changes at this stage. It is no longer income replacement — it is tax efficiency and liquidity. A death benefit passes to heirs generally income-tax-free, immediately, outside probate, and with no ten-year drawdown deadline. Compare that with an inherited IRA, which most non-spouse beneficiaries must empty within ten years at their own marginal rate. For someone with a large pre-tax balance and high-earning children, the after-tax difference to the family can be substantial. Whether it works for you depends on your health, your bracket, and the numbers, which we will run rather than assert.

What is the ten-year rule and does it apply to my children?

Under the SECURE Act, most non-spouse beneficiaries who inherit a retirement account must fully distribute it within ten years of the account owner's death, rather than over their own life expectancy as was previously allowed. Certain eligible designated beneficiaries — a surviving spouse, a minor child of the account owner until majority, a disabled or chronically ill beneficiary, and someone not more than ten years younger — have different treatment. For a typical adult child in their peak earning years, it usually means a large inherited balance is taxed over a compressed window at their highest rates.

Is pension maximisation a good idea or a sales pitch?

It can be either, which is why it deserves careful modelling. The concept is sound: take the higher single-life pension payment and use part of the difference to buy permanent life insurance protecting the surviving spouse. It fails when the coverage is term rather than permanent and expires while both spouses are alive, when the health rating makes the premium uncompetitive, when the plan is underfunded, or when the pension has a subsidised survivor benefit that is hard to beat. We run it both ways, including the scenario where you live to 95 and premiums are still due.

I would rather self-insure long-term care. Is that reasonable?

For some estates, genuinely yes — if the portfolio is large enough that several years of care for both spouses would not meaningfully affect the survivor or the legacy, self-funding is a legitimate choice. The questions worth asking first: what would care actually cost in your area, for how long, for two people rather than one, and what would selling assets to pay for it do to the survivor's income and tax picture? The answer often is that self-insuring works but concentrates a lot of risk on the survivor. Hybrid policies exist largely because they let you hedge that without paying for coverage you may never use.

My term policy expires in three years. What are my options?

Check the contract first for the conversion right — most term policies allow conversion to permanent coverage without new medical underwriting, but the right expires at a stated age or duration. If you are still healthy, you can also shop new coverage and compare. If your health has changed, the conversion right is likely the most valuable thing you own in this context and should be exercised before it lapses. Either way, the decision has a hard date attached, so it is worth putting on the calendar rather than in the drawer.
