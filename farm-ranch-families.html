For farm, ranch and land-owning families

Land-rich and cash-poor is a good problem, right up until the day it isn't.

Ground that has been in the family for generations is worth far more on paper than it produces in cash. When settlement costs come due, the only liquid asset is often the land itself — which is how operations get sold to pay a tax bill.

  • Estate liquidity so nobody has to sell acres to settle the estate
  • A fair outcome between the child who farms and the children who moved away
  • Coverage for operating notes, equipment debt and land loans

The real problem

Why farm estates get broken up

Almost never because the family wanted to sell. Usually because the estate needed cash, the land was the only thing worth anything, and there was no other source.

Appreciated ground, no liquidity

Land bought decades ago at a few hundred dollars an acre can be worth many multiples of that today. The estate is valued at today's number and settled in cash.

One child farms, three do not

The on-farm heir has often worked for below-market wages for twenty years building the operation. The off-farm heirs see an estate divided four ways. Both views are reasonable, and they are incompatible without a plan.

Equal division destroys the operation

Splitting ground four ways among heirs with different intentions produces an operation that cannot be financed, cannot be expanded, and often cannot be farmed at all.

Special-use valuation and installment relief are conditional

Provisions such as special-use valuation for qualifying farm property and installment payment of estate tax attributable to a closely held business exist, but they carry qualification tests and recapture rules that can be violated years later.

Debt does not care about the weather

Operating notes, equipment loans and land mortgages continue through a death, a drought, or a bad price year. Lenders re-underwrite on a change of operator.

The next generation may not want it

Or may want it very much but have no way to buy out siblings, and no lender willing to finance a purchase at appraised value against agricultural cash flow.

Long-term care can consume the ground

Several years of nursing care for one or both parents can absorb an enormous share of a farm balance sheet, and land is what gets sold to pay for it.

Nothing is written down

Farm succession is famously handled by implication — everyone knows who is supposed to get what, and none of it is documented or funded.

How the tools get used

How a policy keeps the ground together

The role of insurance in an agricultural estate is narrow and specific: it creates cash on the day cash is needed, so land does not have to be the source.

Cornerstone

Estate liquidity through a survivorship policy in an ILIT

A second-to-die policy on both parents, owned by an irrevocable life insurance trust, pays after the second death — which is typically when settlement costs actually arrive. Because the trust owns it, the proceeds are generally outside the taxable estate, and the trust can lend to the estate or buy assets from it.

Why it fits: This is the mechanism that lets an operation pay a settlement bill without an auction.
Fairness

Equalising between on-farm and off-farm heirs

The child who farms receives the land and the operating entity. The children who left receive a death benefit of comparable value. Everyone gets a real inheritance, the operation stays whole, and nobody has to borrow against ground to buy out a sibling.

Why it fits: This is the single most common reason farm families come to this conversation, and the one that most reliably keeps a family talking to each other.
Structure

A funded buy-sell inside the family entity

Where the ground or the operation is held in an LLC or a partnership, the operating agreement should set out what happens on a member's death, at what value, and with what funding. Insurance turns that clause into an executable transaction.

Why it fits: Especially important where cousins or in-laws will end up as co-members of the entity.
Debt cover

Term coverage sized to operating and land debt

A straightforward policy sized to the land mortgage, the equipment notes and the typical operating line balance, so the surviving family is not renegotiating credit in the same season they are burying someone.

Why it fits: Inexpensive, uncomplicated, and consistently missing.
Transfer funding

Insuring an installment sale to the on-farm heir

Where the operation is being sold to the next generation over time, coverage on both the buyer and the seller means the note is retired or the estate is made whole if either dies mid-transfer.

Why it fits: Agricultural transfers often run fifteen or twenty years. That is a long window to leave unprotected.
Care costs

Long-term care coverage so care does not cost acres

A hybrid life policy with a long-term care rider, or a standalone policy, funds extended care for a parent without liquidating land. If care is never needed, the death benefit remains for the heirs.

Why it fits: Directly addresses the objection that standalone long-term care premiums feel like money thrown away.
Key person

Coverage on the person who actually runs it

If one individual holds the agronomic knowledge, the landlord relationships and the lender relationship, their loss is an operational and a credit event. Coverage owned by the entity funds the gap.

Why it fits: Landlords who cash-rent to you are watching the same risk, and a documented plan helps keep those leases.
Coordinate

Work with an agricultural attorney and CPA

Entity structure, special-use valuation qualification, installment payment elections, conservation easements, basis and gifting all interact and are genuinely specialised. We fund the plan; the structure has to be designed by people who do agricultural estates regularly.

Why it fits: A general practitioner drafting a farm estate is a common and expensive mistake.

Illustrative examples

What this looks like in practice

Hypothetical situations built to show how the pieces fit together. Yours will be different — that is the point of the call.

The row crop family

Twelve hundred acres, four children, one farmer

Situation
Parents aged 68 and 71, roughly 1,200 acres owned outright, a machinery line and grain facilities, and an operating entity. One son has farmed alongside them for twenty-two years. Three other children live out of state.
Strategy
An agricultural estate attorney restructures ownership so the land and the operating entity pass to the farming son. A survivorship policy on the parents, owned by an irrevocable trust, is sized to give the three off-farm children comparable value, with an additional layer for projected settlement costs.
At the second death, the operation transfers intact to the son who built it, the three off-farm children receive substantial liquid inheritances, and no ground is sold to settle the estate or to buy anyone out. The family entity keeps its lender and its landlord relationships.
The ranch with debt

Protecting an operation through a transition

Situation
Age 57, a cow-calf operation with 1.4 million in land debt and a seasonal operating line. A daughter is returning to the ranch after ten years away and will buy in over fifteen years.
Strategy
Term coverage on the parent sized to the land debt and declining with amortisation, coverage on the daughter sized to the purchase note, and long-term care planning for the parents so the ranch is not the funding source for care.
A death on either side of the transfer does not put the lender in the driver's seat or unwind the buy-in. The daughter's purchase is financeable because the risk of it collapsing mid-term has been removed.

Straight answers

Questions we get from farm & ranch families

Isn't the estate tax exemption high enough that this does not apply to me?

It may be, at the federal level, under current law. Three cautions. Farm ground values have risen sharply and estates that felt modest a decade ago are not modest now. Several states impose their own estate or inheritance tax at much lower thresholds. And the federal exemption has changed several times and is subject to change again. Beyond tax, the equalisation problem — one child farms and the others do not — exists regardless of whether a single dollar of estate tax is owed, and it is what actually breaks most farm families.

What is special-use valuation, and can I count on it?

It is a provision that can allow qualifying farm and ranch real estate to be valued for estate tax purposes based on its agricultural use rather than its highest and best use, which can substantially reduce the taxable value. It comes with strict qualification requirements, a cap on the reduction, and recapture provisions if the property stops qualifying or is sold within a recapture period. It is a real tool and it is not something to build a plan on without an agricultural estate attorney confirming your situation qualifies and will keep qualifying.

How do I treat my kids fairly when only one of them farms?

Start by deciding whether you mean fair or equal, because they are different. The farming child has usually contributed labour and sweat equity for decades at below-market compensation, which many families choose to recognise. The most common workable structure is: the operation and the ground go to the on-farm heir, and life insurance provides comparable liquid value to the off-farm heirs. The critical second step is telling everyone what the plan is while you are alive. Surprises at the reading of a will are what end families.

Can the farm pay the premiums?

Sometimes, depending on the entity structure and what the policy is for. Key person coverage owned by the operating entity is straightforward. Personal coverage for estate equalisation is generally paid personally or through gifts to a trust, and premiums for personal life insurance are not deductible. Where the entity is involved, the ownership and beneficiary structure has to be set up carefully — including employer-owned life insurance notice and consent requirements — to avoid making an otherwise tax-free death benefit taxable. This is a question for your CPA, and we will bring it to them.

We are asset rich but the cash flow is tight. How do we pay for this?

That is the normal starting point, and it shapes the design. Options include starting with term coverage and converting later, funding heavily only in strong price years, using a survivorship policy — which is materially cheaper than two individual policies — and sizing to the actual equalisation gap rather than to a round number. In some cases a portion of cash rent income is redirected to premium. What we would not do is design something that only works if every year is a good year.
