The real problem

Why generic advice fails clinicians

A physician at 35 has the balance sheet of a 22-year-old and the income of a 55-year-old. That combination creates risks most planning templates never contemplate.

A compressed earning window

You started saving eight to twelve years later than your college roommates while they compounded. There is less runway, which makes the efficiency of every dollar matter more.

Debt that does not always die with you

Federal student loans are generally discharged at death. Private and refinanced loans frequently are not, and a co-signing spouse or parent can be left holding a six-figure balance.

Your hands, eyes and back are the asset

A hand surgeon with a tremor, an anesthesiologist with a back injury, a dentist with neuropathy — none of them are disabled in a general sense. They are just done in their specialty. Definitions matter enormously here.

Hospital group coverage is thinner than it looks

Group life is typically one to two times base salary, taxable above the first 50,000 dollars of coverage, and it disappears the day you change employers. Group disability is usually capped and often taxable.

Income that outgrows the tax shelters

Once the 403(b), 457(b), HSA and backdoor Roth are maxed, high earners run out of tax-advantaged room while still in a top bracket.

Liability sits close to the surface

Clinicians think about asset protection differently than most professionals. In many states, life insurance cash value carries creditor protection — the specifics vary and need to be confirmed with your attorney.

Practice ownership adds a second set of risks

If you own a piece of a group, your equity is illiquid, hard to value, and worth very little to your family unless a funded buy-sell agreement exists.

Locums and 1099 work has no safety net

No group life, no group disability, no employer match. Everything is on you to build, which is an opportunity as much as a gap.

How the tools get used

Insurance plays that actually fit a medical career

Not every one of these belongs in your plan. These are the moves we most often end up discussing with clinicians, and why they tend to fit.

Do this first

Own-occupation, specialty-specific disability coverage

A true own-occupation definition pays benefits if you cannot perform the material duties of your specialty, even if you go and do something else for income. Add a residual or partial rider so a reduced schedule still triggers a partial benefit, and a future increase option so coverage can grow with your income without new medical underwriting.

Why it fits: For most clinicians under 50, disability is a far more likely event than death — and it is the risk that group coverage handles worst.
Foundation

A term ladder sized to debt plus dependent years

Rather than one round number, we stack policies: a longer term sized to the years until children are independent, and a shorter one sized to the student loan balance and the mortgage, so coverage steps down as those obligations burn off instead of paying for protection you no longer need.

Why it fits: This is usually the cheapest way for a resident or new attending to get to a genuinely adequate death benefit.
Lock it in early

Convertible term while you are young and healthy

Buying convertible term as a resident or fellow lets you convert some or all of it to permanent coverage later without proving insurability again. A single diagnosis, a positive family history, or a new medication can change your rating permanently.

Why it fits: Insurability is an asset that quietly depreciates. Clinicians, of all people, tend to appreciate this argument.
Tax diversification

A Life Insurance Retirement Plan (LIRP) after the qualified plans are full

An overfunded whole life or indexed universal life policy, structured deliberately to stay inside IRS limits and avoid Modified Endowment Contract status, can build cash value on a tax-deferred basis and later provide supplemental income through policy loans. This is a supplement to the 403(b) and Roth, never a replacement for them.

Why it fits: The argument here is about tax diversification in retirement, not about beating the market. If your entire retirement is pre-tax, a future rate increase hits you at full strength.
Practice owners

A funded buy-sell and key person coverage for the group

If you are a partner in a practice, the partnership agreement should specify what happens on death or disability — and there should be money behind it. Cross-purchase and entity-purchase structures have different tax and basis consequences; that choice should be made deliberately with your CPA, not by default.

Why it fits: Without funding, the surviving partners either buy your family out with cash they do not have, or the family becomes an unwilling partner.
Retention

Executive bonus (Section 162) plans for associate physicians

The practice pays the premium on a policy owned by a key associate as a deductible bonus. Simple to install compared to a formal non-qualified plan, and it gives an associate a reason to stay through a vesting schedule.

Why it fits: Recruiting a replacement physician can cost a practice hundreds of thousands in lost production. Retention tools are cheap by comparison.
Later-career

Chronic illness and long-term care acceleration riders

A rider that lets you access part of the death benefit early for a qualifying chronic illness. Many carriers now offer these at modest cost inside a permanent policy, so the money is not wasted if care is never needed.

Why it fits: Clinicians see the cost and the family strain of long-term care up close. They rarely need to be convinced this risk is real.
Liquidity

Cash value as a source of capital for practice or property

Accumulated cash value can be borrowed against, generally without a credit check or bank underwriting, and can be assigned as collateral for a practice acquisition or a commercial real estate purchase.

Why it fits: Useful for the physician who wants to buy into a surgery center, an imaging partnership, or a building without disturbing invested assets.

Illustrative examples

What this looks like in practice

Hypothetical situations built to show how the pieces fit together. Yours will be different — that is the point of the call.

The new attending

From resident to attending without leaving a gap

Situation
Age 34, finishing an anesthesiology fellowship. 240,000 in refinanced private student loans, a spouse who has been the sole earner, and an income about to go from 70,000 to 400,000.
Strategy
True own-occupation disability with a future increase rider locked in before the income jump, a 30-year convertible term policy sized to income replacement, a 15-year term sized to the loan balance, and a modest permanent policy started small and funded more heavily once cash flow stabilises.
The disability policy is underwritten while young and healthy, and the future increase option lets the benefit rise with income without new medical exams. If the loans outlive the earner, they do not land on the co-signing spouse. The permanent policy becomes the tax-diversified bucket once the 403(b) and backdoor Roth are maxed.
The practice partner

Making a partnership agreement mean something

Situation
Age 48, a one-third partner in a dermatology group. The operating agreement requires the practice to buy out a deceased partner at a formula value, but nothing is set aside to do it.
Strategy
Policies structured to fund the buy-sell obligation, sized to the current formula value with a review cadence as the practice grows, plus key person coverage on the highest-producing partner.
If a partner dies, the buyout is funded on day one rather than out of future profits. The family gets a fair price in cash, the surviving partners keep control, and the bank does not get nervous about the practice line of credit.

These are hypothetical examples for illustration only, not guarantees, projections, or recommendations for any specific policy or person. Straight answers

Questions we get from doctors, nurses & specialists

I already have disability and life insurance through the hospital. Is that enough?

Usually not, for three reasons. Group life is typically one to two times base salary, which rarely matches what a family actually needs. Group disability is often capped at a dollar amount that undershoots a specialist income, uses a weaker definition of disability, and is taxable to you if the employer pays the premium. And neither one follows you when you change jobs. Group coverage is a good base layer; individual coverage is what makes the plan portable and adequate.

What happens to my student loans if I die?

Federal student loans are generally discharged on the borrower's death. Private loans and loans that have been refinanced with a private lender frequently are not, and a spouse or parent who co-signed can remain fully liable. This is one of the single most common uncovered exposures we find with residents and new attendings, and term coverage sized to the balance is inexpensive at that age.

Is life insurance a good investment for a high-income physician?

Framed that way, usually no. Permanent life insurance is not a substitute for a diversified portfolio and it should not compete with employer match, HSA, or Roth space. Where it earns a place is as a tax-diversification and liquidity tool after the qualified plans are full — a bucket that grows tax-deferred, can be accessed through loans, and pays a death benefit to heirs largely income-tax-free. We will show you the trade-offs, including the costs and the scenarios where it does not make sense.

I am a resident and money is tight. Should I wait?

Waiting costs you the two things that are cheapest right now: your age and your health. A convertible term policy and a small disability policy with a future increase option can be locked in on a resident budget and expanded later without new underwriting. That is the specific reason to act before the income arrives rather than after.

Does life insurance cash value protect assets from a malpractice claim?

In many states, life insurance cash value and death benefits carry some level of creditor protection, but the rules vary significantly by state and by who owns the policy. This should never be the primary reason you buy a policy, and it must be confirmed with an attorney licensed in your state. We will flag where it may apply and refer the question to counsel.
